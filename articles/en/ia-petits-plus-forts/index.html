Does AI really make the little ones stronger... or just more dependent?

AI promises small structures the power of a multinational. But behind this promise of emancipation lies a more nuanced reality: gaining strength today could mean losing autonomy tomorrow. .

The idea that Artificial Intelligence acts as a "great equalizer" has become one of the pillars of contemporary technological discourse. For small structures — startups, SMEs or independents — the promise is attractive: AI would offer a team of three people the strike power of a multinational. By automating tasks ungrateful, analyzing previously unreadable data volumes and generating content At the speed of light, AI would allow the "small" to challenge the giants on their own land.

Yet behind this narrative of emancipation lies a more nuanced reality, or even paradoxical. If AI does provide small players with new capabilities, it simultaneously locks them in a technological structure of which they do not possess neither the keys nor the infrastructure. The immediate gain of force would it not be prelude to sustainable submission?

I. Archimedes lever: Why AI strengthens small

It would be dishonest to deny the incredible power gain that AI brings to light structures. Historically, the size of a company was correlated with its size. ability to mobilize human capital for support functions: marketing, accounting, customer service, legal research. AI breaks this glass ceiling in several ways.

1. The collapse of marginal costs of expertise

Previously, conducting a serious market research or designing an advertising campaign high quality involved recruiting or outsourcing at gold prices. Today, tools such as free LLM or image generators to obtain a professional result for the price of a derisory monthly subscription. This democratisation of expertise allows small children to reallocate their resources to their core business.

2. Agility as a weapon of war

Large companies are often hampered by bureaucratic inertia. For implementing a new AI tool, a multinational must pass through months of validation and mass training committees. On the other hand, an independent person may rotate in one afternoon. "Small" use AI to test hypotheses, launch MVPs and adjust their strategy in real time — a speed that capital Only can't buy.

3. Mass customization

One of the great historical advantages of giants was their ability to deal with millions of clients via standardized processes. AI allows children to do the opposite: to treat each customer in an ultra-personalised way thanks to automation Smart, without increasing the payroll. A craftsman can now manage a global customer relationship with the same care as a neighborhood trader, thanks to conversational agents and CRM tools doped with AI.

II. The structural dependency trap

However, this new force is not intrinsic to the "small". She's rented. And this is where the notion of dependency appears. Unlike revolutions in which one could own its own steam machine, the AI revolution is based on a centralized infrastructure.

1. Technological feudality

Almost all AI tools used by small players rely on the infrastructures of some giants (Microsoft/OpenAI, Google, Amazon, Anthropic). SMEs do not build their AI; They call APIs. This means that the heart beating their competitive advantage belongs to a third party. If the supplier changes its prices or changes its conditions, the "small" can see its business model collapse overnight. It's a form of digital metayage: we grow His land, but the tools belong to the local lord.

2. Atrophy of internal skills

By massively delegating reflection, writing or code to AI, small structures run the risk of skill erosion. If a code startup recruits only "AI pilots" unable to understand deep layers What will happen if the system fails? Dependence is no longer only technical, it becomes cognitive.

Open-source alternatives such as Stable Diffusion or Mistral allow partially free themselves from this dependence. However, they require technical skills that most small structures do not have Again.

3. Standardisation of innovation

If everyone uses the same AI models to write their e-mails, design their logos or code its applications, the distinction by quality fades. The little ones are enclosed in an "average" generated by algorithms. AI, in A small actor of the stand out by a craft singularity or a unique voice.

III. The data: the new strategic gap

The real stake is not in the interface of tools, nor in power gross models, but on a more discreet terrain: the data. AI is not Magic. It's statistical. And to be relevant, she needs fuel. constant: structured, contextualized, usable data.

This is where the ditch is growing.

For technology giants, AI works as a positive retroactive loop. The more clients they have, the more interactions they collect. The more they collect The more their models improve. This self-reinforced mechanism creates a Cumulative benefit almost exponential. The scale turns quantity into quality.

Small players, on the other hand, are evolving in another regime. Their data volume is limited by nature: fewer customers, less history, less diversity behavioral. Worse still, they often contribute — without the will — to enrich the platforms they use. Each request sent to an API feeds indirectly the supplier ecosystem. They consume global intelligence, but produce little proprietary intelligence.

In this context, equal access to tools masks unequal accumulation.

The vertical AI track

The pathway is what can be called vertical AI. The "little" stops trying to compete in volume and chooses specialization. He doesn't use more AI as an interchangeable generalist tool, but as an amplifier a specific knowledge that he alone has.

The key then becomes the owner data.

  • Craft data from years of practice
  • Industrial maintenance history impossible to reproduce
  • Clinical files structured in a medical practice
  • Accurately documented B2B customer relations
  • Internal processes optimized over time

These data are not massive on a global scale, but they are of a density exceptional strategic in their niche. By adjusting smaller models and on this exclusive raw material, the small actor builds a barrier the entry that a giant cannot cross without access to this information. To this stage, AI changes in nature: it is no longer a leased dependence on subscription, It becomes a structuring asset.

IV. Towards strategic autonomy?

So the question is not whether AI makes it strong or dependent, but how navigate between the two. The autonomy of the "small" in the AI era rests on three pillars:

1. Hybridization

Using AI for productivity while maintaining human expertise challenge and supervise the machine. The total delegation is the trap; the active supervision is output.

2. Interoperability

Do not bind to a single supplier. Use open-source templates when it is possible, in order to keep hands on its production tool. The diversification of suppliers reduce vulnerability to tariff policy changes.

3. Data sovereignty

Ensure that the data that make the value of the business is not simply offered to global models but used to strengthen internal know-how. It is the only lasting defence against technological feudalism.

Conclusion

AI is a force multiplier. But in mathematics, if you multiply a by an external factor, the result depends entirely on the permanence of this Factor. For small actors, AI is a performance drug: it gives the illusion of being a giant, but it creates an addiction to the infrastructures of the real giants.

The little one really gets stronger when he uses AI to free up time so doing what a machine can never do: creating deep human ties, take ethical risks and cultivate an extraordinary intuition. It just becomes more dependent if it is merely the human interface of a proprietary algorithm.

Ultimately, AI does not automatically redistribute power cards. The real strength does not lie in access to AI — because everyone will have access to it — but in the ability to use it without dissolving itself.
