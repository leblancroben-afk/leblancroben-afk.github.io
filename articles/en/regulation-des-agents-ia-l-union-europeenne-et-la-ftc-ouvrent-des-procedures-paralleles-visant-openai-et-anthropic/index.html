Regulation of AI agents: the European Union and the FTC open parallel procedures for OpenAI and Anthropic

The European Union and the FTC are opening parallel procedures for OpenAI and Anthropic, following several incidents involving AI agents from their test environments.

Artificial intelligence agents capable of using tools, navigating the Internet and carrying out actions in several stages now attract the attention of regulators. In October 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic found themselves at the centre of requests for information on the safety of their systems and the risks associated with their autonomy.

According to information reported by several media, the European Commission has asked the two companies in the context of its work on the European AI Regulation. In particular, the authorities seek to understand how models are assessed, what measures prevent dangerous behaviour and how companies react when an agent exceeds the prescribed limits.

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) also reportedly issued formal requests for information, known as the Civil Investigative Claims. These procedures allow authorities to collect documents and information in the course of their investigations. They alone do not constitute proof of an infringement.

Attention to autonomous agents is explained by a significant difference with conventional conversational assistants. An agent can chain multiple operations, use software and interact with external services. If its authorizations are poorly defined or its control mechanisms fail, its actions may go beyond the original framework.

The incidents referred to in the cited articles include security assessments in which officers interacted with resources outside the authorized perimeter. The case attributed to OpenAI and involving Hugging Face, however, deserves to be seen in its context: one must distinguish an action observed during a controlled test from a confirmed malicious access to a third-party system.

For developers, these questions have very concrete consequences. An agent used to modify code, manage files or perform online operations must have limited authorizations, a searchable activity log and mechanisms to block risky action. Tests must also check what happens when the system receives conflicting instructions or encounters an unexpected situation.

The issue for regulators is whether the safety features announced by companies are adapted to the real capabilities of their agents. For AI actors, these requests could strengthen documentation, evaluation and supervision requirements prior to the deployment of more autonomous systems.

At this stage, requests for information, regulatory investigation and sanction should not be confused. The exact scope of these procedures will depend on the documents reviewed and the conclusions of the authorities. However, one thing is clear: as AI agents become more empowered, the question of their control becomes as important as that of their performance.
